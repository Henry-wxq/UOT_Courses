&=\frac{\partial}{\partial y'}\!\Bigl(-(y')^2(1+y')^2\Bigr) \\
&=-\frac{\partial}{\partial y'}\!\Bigl((y')^2(1+y')^2\Bigr) \\
&= -\Bigl(2y'(1+y')^2 + (y')^2 \cdot 2(1+y')\Bigr) \\
&= -\Bigl(2y' + 4(y')^2 + 2(y')^3 + 2(y')^2 + 2(y')^3\Bigr) \\
&= - 2y' - 6(y')^2 - 4(y')^3
\end{align*}

Thus, for $f_{y'y'2}$, we have,
$$f_{y'y'2} = - 12 (y')^2 - 12y' - 2  = - 12\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)^2 - 12\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right) - 2 = 1 > 0 $$

To show it has no conjugate points, I'll solve the general solution from Euler Lagrange equation, which, 
\begin{align*}
	\frac{d}{dx} \bigl(- 2y' - 6(y')^2 - 4(y')^3 \bigr) &= 0 \\
	\implies -2y'' - 12y'y'' - 12(y')^2y'' &= 0 \\
	\implies \bigl(2 - 12y' - 12(y')^2\bigr)\,y'' &= 0 \\
	\implies 1 \cdot y'' &= 0\\
	\implies y'' &= 0 \quad \text{(We obtain the general solution)}\\
	\implies y &= C_1x + C_2 
\end{align*}

Thus, $u_1(x)$ and $u_2(x)$ are given by, 
$$u_1 = \frac{\partial y}{\partial C_1} = x \quad \quad u_2 = \frac{\partial y}{\partial C_2} = 1$$

We have, $x_0 = 0$. If $\mathcal K$ is conjugate point to 0, get, 
\begin{align*}
	u_2(\mathcal K)u_1(x_0) &= u_2(x_0)u_1(\mathcal K) \\
	\implies u_2(\mathcal K)u_1(0) &= u_2(0)u_1(\mathcal K) \\
	\implies 0 &= \mathcal K = x_0
\end{align*}

Since $x_0 = \mathcal K = 0$, there's no conjugate points to $x_0$.

Thus, from the textbook\footnote{The Calculus of Variations by van Brunt, Page 242.}, Theorem 10.5.1, since $f_{y'y'2} > 0$ and there's no conjugate points to $x_0$, $-\mathcal J$ has a weak local minimum in $S$ at $\bar y$ with $c = -\frac{1}{2}$.

Therefore, form the textbook\footnote{The Calculus of Variations by van Brunt, Page 241. The Textbook states that: $J$ is said to have a weak local maximum at $y \in S$ if $-J$ has a weak local minimum at $y$. }, Section 10.5, $\mathcal J$ has a weak local maximum in $S$ at $\bar y$ with $c = -\frac{1}{2}$.
\hfill $\blacksquare$

\vspace{10pt}
\noindent
\textbf{(e) WTS: $\bar y$ is a global minimum if $c=\frac{1}{6}$.}

Suppose $c=\frac{1}{6}$, which $y' = c = \frac{1}{6}$.

From lecture slide\footnote{July 8th Lecture Slides}, let $\hat y = \bar y + \epsilon \eta$ be a perturbation where $\eta \in H = \{\eta \in X: \eta(x_0) =\eta(0)0,\, \eta(x_1) = \eta(1) = 0 \}$, gives, 
\begin{align*}
	J(\hat y) - J(\bar y) &= J(y + \epsilon \eta) - J(\bar{y}) \\
	&= \int_0^1 \Big((c+\epsilon \eta')^2 (1+c+\epsilon \eta')^2\Big) dx - \int_0^1 \Big(c^2(1+c)^2 \Big) dx \\
	&= \int_0^1 \Big((c+\epsilon \eta')^2 (1+c+\epsilon \eta')^2 - c^2(1+c)^2 \Big) dx \\
	&= \int_0^1 \Big((c^2+\epsilon^2 (\eta')^2 +2c\epsilon \eta' ) (1+c+\epsilon \eta')^2 - c^2(1+c)^2\Big) dx \\
	&= \int_0^1 \Big(\big(c^2 + 2c\epsilon \eta' + \epsilon^2 (\eta')^2\big)\,\big((1+c)^2 + 2(1+c)\epsilon \eta' + \epsilon^2 (\eta')^2\big) - c^2(1+c)^2\Big) dx \\
	&= \int_0^1 2c(1+c)^2 \epsilon \eta' + (6c^2+6c+1)\epsilon^2 (\eta')^2 + (4c+2)\epsilon^3 (\eta')^3 + \epsilon^4 (\eta')^4 dx \\
	&= \int_0^1 2c(1+c)^2 \epsilon \eta' dx + \int_0^1 (6c^2+6c+1)\epsilon^2 (\eta')^2 dx + \int_0^1 (4c+2)\epsilon^3 (\eta')^3 dx + \int_0^1 \epsilon^4 (\eta')^4 dx 
\end{align*}

Since $\eta(0)=\eta(1)=0$, we have, $\int_0^1\eta'(x)\,dx=\eta(1)-\eta(0)=0$, which the first-order term is cancelled. I'll then substitute $c=\frac{1}{6}$, gives, 
\begin{align*}
	J(\hat y) - J(\bar y) &= \int_0^1 (6(\frac{1}{6})^2+6(\frac{1}{6})+1)\epsilon^2 (\eta')^2 dx + \int_0^1 (4(\frac{1}{6})+2)\epsilon^3 (\eta')^3 dx + \int_0^1 \epsilon^4 (\eta')^4 dx \\
	&= \int_0^1 \frac{13}{6}\epsilon^2 (\eta')^2 dx + \int_0^1 \frac{8}{3}\epsilon^3 (\eta')^3 dx + \int_0^1 \epsilon^4 (\eta')^4 dx \\
	&= \epsilon^2 \int_0^1 (\eta')^2 \Big( \epsilon^2 (\eta')^2 + \frac{8}{3}\epsilon \eta' + \frac{13}{6} \Big) dx \\
	&= \epsilon^2 \int_0^1 (\eta')^2 \Big( (\epsilon \eta' + \frac{4}{3})^2 + \frac{7}{18} \Big) dx
\end{align*}

Since all the terms except the constant $\frac{7}{18}$ are non-negative, gives, the integral is non-negative, which, 
$$J(\hat y) - J(\bar y) = \epsilon^2 \int_0^1 (\eta')^2 \Big( (\epsilon \eta' + \frac{4}{3})^2 + \frac{7}{18} \Big) dx \ge 0$$

Therefore, I've shown that $\mathcal J$ has a global minimum in $S$ at $\bar y$ with $c = \frac{1}{6}$.

\hfill $\blacksquare$
